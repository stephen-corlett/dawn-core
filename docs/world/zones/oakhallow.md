# Zone brief: Oakhallow

The human starting zone, levels 1 to 10. Older gate log entries call it `first-zone`. See
[the zone pipeline](../../zone-pipeline.md). Keep this short, because a brief that needs reading
twice won't be followed.

## Premise

Gate 1, passed. This is premise D, the half-cut oak, changed as the Decisions say.

- **Request:** "Design the first zone of the game." This is where a new player starts, on foot at
  6.4 m/s, with a 15 to 30 minute natural route. The signed-off art bible already has a forest mood
  (warm low sun through haze, muted greens, warm earth), so at least one premise should use it, but
  the options don't all have to. The bible's high fantasy, grounded tone applies. Because no zone is
  before it, it also has to establish the world and show a player that following what they see pays
  off.
- **Theme:** Peasants struggle with hunger, theft, and fearsome creatures. The army is away at war
  and has no time for trouble at home, so aspiring adventurers step in.
- **What happened here:** These hamlets have always lived off the wood, fattening pigs on acorns and
  grinding acorn flour in lean years. Three years ago the crown's timber gangs came for keels and
  siege engines, stripped the oaks from the hillsides, and hauled them to the city. Then they started
  on the great oak, the oldest tree anyone knows of, and were marched to the war before they finished.
  - **What the felling left:** Ragged fells of grey stumps and bramble on the hillsides, between dark
    blocks of standing wood. Rotting log stacks that nobody came back for. An empty felling camp.
  - **What the hauling left:** The log road, rutted deep, running out of the wood towards the city.
  - **What the unfinished cut left:** One huge wedge in the great oak's trunk, with the scaffolds
    still against it.
  - **What the lost acorns left:** Broken pens, and empty smokehouses. Ground rooted up along the
    wood's edge by pigs that were let go because nobody could feed them.
- **What's happening now:** The great oak bears the last big crop of acorns, and every hamlet depends
  on it. Stores are low, the road to the city isn't safe, and no help is coming.
- **Threads:** Each stands alone, in any order.
  - **Hunger:** Pigs turned loose have gone wild and grown huge, and they're rooting up the last seed
    patches. A swineherd wants her best sow back before she's lost for good.
  - **Theft:** Deserters from the timber gang live in the felling camp, and raid hamlets for what
    little is stored.
  - **Creatures:** The felling drove something big out of the old wood, and it now sleeps by the log
    road and takes a pig a week.
  - **People:** Children gathering acorns around the great oak have to go farther each day, and one
    family's haven't come home.
- **The way through:** A new character starts at a swineherd's home at the back of the wood, about as
  far from the city as the zone goes. It's one house and a broken pen, not the main town. Pig bells
  and a worn path lead to the first fell, which shows the great oak and, in the fold below, the smoke
  of the main town, which is small. The zone then runs across the fells, up to the oak's high ground,
  and down the log road. A player reaches the city near the far end.
- **The tentpole:** The great oak, a plain old tree with no glow, about 120 m tall on high ground. A
  player steps out of dim wood onto a bright felled hillside and sees it standing alone across the
  fells. The wedge in its side only shows on the walk up.
- **The city:** A distant shape, with only a direction and a rough size. It lies past the zone's far
  end, the way the log road runs, and it's about 1 km across with walls and towers about 60 m tall. It
  first shows from the oak's high ground, as a long, low band with smoke.
- **The question:** How will the people living under the great oak get through the winter?
- **Who lives here:**
  - **Hamlet families:** They want to reach spring. They've fenced seed patches among the stumps, and
    worn paths from each hamlet to the oak.
  - **Deserters:** They want food, and not to be sent back to the war. They've left smoke over the
    camp, and broken stores behind them.
  - **Wild pigs:** They want food, and root up the wood's edge and the seed patches.
  - **The thing from the old wood:** It wants a new home, and has made the log road dangerous.
- **The feeling:** People need you here, and the world is bigger than you knew. It goes from shelter
  in the wood, to loss on the fells, to awe at the oak, to the pull of the city ahead.
- **Place in the world:** The first human zone, with a fixed level range, 6.4 m/s on foot, and the
  human city at its far end. The proposed row in [the atlas](../atlas.md) is in the gate log.
- **Mood:** The art bible's forest: warm, low sun through haze, deep but readable shadow, muted greens,
  and warm earth. Standing wood is dim and close. Fells are brighter, with grey stumps, bramble, and
  thinner haze so the far view works, which needs fog that follows a place. Haze stays warm, because
  [the art bible](../../art/bible.md) rejected white, even light.

#### Premise A: The held evening

Forest, using the signed-off mood.

- **What happened here:** In a winter that wouldn't end, three centuries ago, the valley's
  sun-wardens raised a needle of black stone and pinned the sun above it. It has been the same late
  afternoon here ever since. Thirty years ago the needle's shadow began to creep, a hand's width a
  year.
- **What the pinning left:** The needle. A dead-straight strip of bare ground where its shadow lay,
  running out through the forest. Every tree leaning the same way, towards the sun. Lit slopes that
  are orchards all year, and slopes facing away locked in frost-bound woods nobody can enter, which
  give the zone its blocked land.
- **What the creep left:** Dated tally posts stepping sideways across the strip. Saplings along one
  edge, and trees dying along the other.
- **The tension:** Orchard families need the sun held. People on the cold side want it to move.
- **The question:** Why has the sun stopped, and what happens when it moves?
- **The sight:** The bare strip running straight through the trees to a black needle. Then, on
  reaching the end of its shadow, the sun sitting exactly on its point.
- **How it teaches:** Forest hides everything, so the strip is the one long view, and it's a line a
  player already wants to walk. Its posts lead to the hub, and a player who looks at them sees them
  step sideways, so the first thing they notice is also the zone's story.
- **What it says about the world:** People once commanded the sky, and their descendants live with
  what they did.
- **Costs and open issues:**
  - It reuses the forest look, trees, and rocks, and needs new needle, orchard, and settlement kits.
  - A held sun means no day and night here, and walking into the next zone has to move the sun.
  - At today's sun, about 26 degrees up, a 240 m needle throws about 485 m of shadow. A 3 km shadow
    needs a sun about 5 degrees up, which changes the signed-off look. Otherwise the strip is bare
    ground, and only its last 485 m is in real shadow. The sun sits on the point either way.
  - A needle 20 m wide is 6 pixels at 3 km, so it needs a broad, wedged base to read.

#### Premise B: The carcass town

Burned pine uplands, not the forest mood.

- **What happened here:** Forty years ago a dragon came down burning across these hills and died on
  the high ground, with an iron bolt as big as a mill tower through its neck. Nobody here fired it.
  People came to strip the body the way whalers strip a whale, and their camp became the town.
- **What the fall left:** A burned furrow about 4 km long through the pines, now grey snags and
  fireweed. A trench too deep to cross. A river that runs rust below the body.
- **What the stripping left:** Ribs on the skyline, cut away in steps. Smoke from the rendering
  pits. A cart road worn from the carcass to the town.
- **What the bolt left:** The bolt itself, still in the neck, pointing back the way it flew.
- **The tension:** The town lives on bone, and the bone is running out. The hill clans lost their
  grazing to the burn, and say the body is poisoning their river.
- **The question:** What could kill that, and is it still out there?
- **The sight:** A ribcage on the skyline, taller than any tower, with cranes and smoke working on
  it.
- **How it teaches:** The furrow is a wide scar that a player can't miss, and it runs towards the
  thing that made it. Following it brings the ribs over a crest, then the town's smoke. Lining up
  the bolt later points at the next zone, so the same habit pays off twice.
- **What it says about the world:** Dragons are real, something out there kills them, and ordinary
  people turn wonders into work.
- **Costs and open issues:**
  - It reuses the pines and rocks, and needs new burned land, town, and carcass kits.
  - It needs its own clear, cold mood: slate sky, ash, bone, and rust, with little haze.
  - The body's whole outline does the work at 3 km, at about 170 pixels for 600 m. A 15 m rib is
    only 4 pixels, so it needs a few huge ribs, not many thin ones.
  - To stay Grounded it has to be bone, timber, and smoke, with no gore.

#### Premise C: The sea that left

The floor of a dead sea, not the forest mood.

- **What happened here:** Three generations ago the sea drew out in one night and never came back.
  Ships settled where they were, a port was left on its cliff, and the city of a swimming people
  lay open to the sky, empty. The lighthouse on its rock kept burning.
- **What the draining left:** The old sea cliff and its tide-marked terraces, which block and shape
  the land. Salt flats and dry trenches. Giant kelp dried into a pale standing wood. Wrecks with
  their bows all pointing the way the water went.
- **What the exposure left:** A city with doors in its roofs and no stairs. A port with its piers
  hanging in the air, and ladders down to the salvage.
- **The tension:** The port lives on salt and salvage from the city. The old harbor families still
  keep the tide tables and say the water is coming back. Nobody admits to tending the lamp.
- **The question:** Who is still lighting a lighthouse for a sea that's gone?
- **The sight:** Looking up from the seabed at a town on a cliff, with its piers overhead.
- **How it teaches:** A player arrives on the sea floor, and the nearest wreck is an odd shape
  that's close enough to reach at once. Walking to it shows the next hull and the piers above, so
  the hub is found from below. Wrecks that all point one way become a compass the map doesn't have.
- **What it says about the world:** Older peoples lived here, and the world can change overnight.
- **Costs and open issues:**
  - It reuses no land art, so it needs new sea-floor, port, and sunken-city kits, and a new mood.
    The light has to stay warm and low, because the art bible rejected white, even light.
  - It's the only option with no forest and no water to draw, so its far view is likely the
    cheapest. That's unmeasured.
  - Flat land shows everything at once, so reefs and terraces must do the hiding that "Reveal, don't
    list" asks for.

  forest rule asks. The path opens onto the first fell, which shows the oak and, in the fold below,
  the hamlet's smoke. So the first thing a player follows pays off twice, and each later fell shows
  more. The city only shows from the highest fell.
- **How forests stay enclosed:** Standing wood shows nothing far away. Every long view is from a fell,
  and these are ragged patches on slopes, so they aren't a line to walk like premise A's strip.
- **Costs and open issues:**
  - It uses the signed-off look, trees, and rocks. It needs a hero oak, and new stump, hamlet, and
    camp kits.
  - At 220 m tall and 180 m wide, the oak is about 64 by 52 pixels at 3 km. A 40 m trunk is only 12
    pixels, so the crown does the work. Seeing it over 15 m trees takes about 205 m of open fell.
  - Its outline is close to Elden Ring's great tree. It has to stay a plain old oak, with no glow.
  - The fells need a clearer view than the 85 m fog of today's forest, so it depends on fog that
    follows a place.

#### Premise E: The Sower on the hill

Chalk downs and grain fields, not the forest mood. **The hunger:** Plenty grows here, and it's taken
away.

- **What happened here:** This is the city's breadbasket. Long ago the first farmers cut a giant
  figure of a sower into the chalk, and every village has scoured it clean each harvest since. Two
  years ago the crown's purveyors set a war quota. The ploughmen marched, and the grain that's left
  goes up the road for the army. The people who grow it are going hungry beside it.
- **What the old farming left:** The Sower. Stepped field banks along the slopes. Pits where chalk
  was dug to sweeten the fields, now steep white hollows.
- **What the quota left:** Barns with the crown's seal on their doors. A grain road rutted deep by
  carts. Half the fields gone to thistle, with ploughs left where they stopped.
- **What the missing hands left:** The Sower going green from the feet up, because too few people
  came to scour it. A player can see how bad things are from 3 km away.
- **Threads:**
  - **Hunger:** Gleaners have been ordered off the stubble by a quota clerk, and a widow wants help
    getting her family through to spring.
  - **Theft:** Grain is leaving a sealed barn at night. The old bailiff guarding it needs the thief
    caught, and the thief is one of his neighbors.
  - **Creatures:** Griffons have nested in the big chalk pit, and with no men on the down they take a
    lamb a day.
  - **People:** Rustlers are driving off a shepherd's flock through the dry valleys, and selling the
    meat at the city gate.
  - **Custom:** The few old women still scouring the Sower camp on the hill, and need someone with
    them when night comes.
- **The sight:** A pale giant striding across a green hillside, half gone under the grass, with the
  grain carts crawling along below it.
- **How it teaches:** The figure is visible from the first rise, and a player wants to know what it
  is. The grain road runs towards it, then bends away, and the hub is in a dry valley that the road
  doesn't enter. Its tower and smoke only show from the bend, so taking the side track is the first
  discovery. From the Sower's head, the city and every region of the zone are in view at once.
- **Costs and open issues:**
  - It reuses little: rocks, and trees for small hillside woods, which stay enclosed. It needs a new
    mood of straw gold and chalk under the same warm, low sun, and new farm and barn kits.
  - It's the cheapest tentpole, because it's painted on the ground. But the ground has to carry it,
    and that's unproven at this size.
  - On a 25 degree slope, a figure 350 m long rises about 148 m, so it's about 43 by 64 pixels at 3
    km for a 220 m width. A 30 m limb is only 9 pixels, so it has to be a filled shape, not an outline.
  - Downs are easy to walk on, so reaching 40% blocked land is the hard part. Chalk cliffs, a river
    with few crossings, and thick hillside woods have to do it.
  - The quota is the crown's doing, so this option says the most about how the kingdom treats its
    peasants. A person should say if that's the right tone for the human starting zone.

#### Premise F: The stopped mill

Drowned fen and reed beds, not the forest mood. **The hunger:** The fields are under water.

- **What happened here:** Generations ago, drainers turned this fen into the best black soil in the
  country, with dykes, straight channels, and a great wind-driven pump as tall as a hill. It took
  skilled hands to keep it running. Two winters ago the millwrights and dyke-reeves were called up.
  A storm broke a sail, nobody could fix it, and the water came back.
- **What the draining left:** The mill. Long dykes standing above the land, and the few raised roads
  that everyone now walks on.
- **What the water left:** Hedge tops and fence posts in lines across still water. Barns and
  farmhouses standing in it to their windows, a plough stranded on a bank, and a breached dyke with
  the water still moving through it.
- **What people did next:** Stilt huts and punts. Eel traps along old field drains. Rye growing on
  dyke tops, because that's the only dry soil left.
- **Threads:**
  - **Hunger:** An old farmer won't leave his flooded house, or the seed rye in its loft, and his
    daughter wants him and the rye out.
  - **Theft:** Thieves are stripping the mill's bronze fittings to sell in the city. The millwright's
    apprentice has stayed to guard it alone.
  - **Creatures:** The flood brought big, low, patient things up from the deep meres. One has taken a
    drowned farm, and the eel-catchers won't go near it.
  - **Toll:** Deserters hold the one causeway to the far dyke, and charge for it in food.
  - **People:** Someone is lifting the eel traps at night, and the catchers each blame a different
    family.
- **The sight:** A windmill as tall as a hill, stopped, with one sail broken, standing over miles of
  gold reed and still water, with the city a dark band on the horizon behind it.
- **How it teaches:** Reed paths are enclosed, and each climb onto a dyke opens the view, so a player
  learns that going up shows the next thing. The hub stands on a hump of old firm ground behind the
  reeds. Its smoke shows over them from the dyke, and reaching it means finding the way through.
  Hedge lines in the water give a player a route to read before they walk it.
- **Costs and open issues:**
  - There's no water in the game, and this option needs a lot of it. It's the largest new feature of
    the three, and it reuses only rocks.
  - It needs a new mood of pewter water and gold reed. Mist has to stay warm, because the art bible
    rejected white, even light.
  - At 195 m to the sail tip, the mill is about 56 pixels at 3 km. A 50 m tower is 14 pixels and a 25
    m sail is 7, so sails must be solid, and it needs testing that the broken sail reads.
  - Blocked land is easy, and easy to see, because it's water. Hiding is the hard part. Dykes, reeds
    taller than a player, and wet woods have to stop everything showing at once.

#### How the route fits

A 25-minute natural route is 9.6 km on foot, about 6.9 km in a straight line at the bible's winding

width carries it, and its height alone wouldn't. These numbers are unverified until gate 2.

"Far from the city" therefore means about 4 to 8 km in a straight line, or 15 to 30 minutes' walk.

#### Who built the palisade, proposed

to the crown, so each load had to be counted out through one gate. The rise is too gentle to stop a
cart going round a lone gate, so they ran a fence between the steep ground on either side, using oak
they were already cutting.

stands just past the crest. When the gangs were marched to the war they left through the gate, and
barred it behind them from the city side, to keep anyone carting off the crown's stacked oak. Nobody
has been back. It took no soldiers, and nobody guards it now, so it fits "no help is coming".
